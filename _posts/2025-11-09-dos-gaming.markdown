I installed an old MS-DOS game on my computer and it got me thinking about the intricacies of retro PC gaming and art preservation in the digital age.

In today's always-online world, resplendent with cheap storage and digital pack-rats, it would be easy to assume that any piece of media - especially something as intricate and comprehensive as a commercial video game - would be preserved eternally. Unfortunately, that is not the case. Thanks to the walled gardens of some modern gaming platforms, which use a mix of online DRM and encryption to keep games from being extracted from where they are "allowed" to be played, we've [had prestigious AAA demos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P.T._(video_game)#Removal_and_remakes) and [iconic indie mobile hits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flappy_Bird#Discontinuation) simply disappear from the world. Of course, this has been an issue for as long as internet-connected gameplay has been a thing; there are [Sega Genesis](https://info.sonicretro.org/Sonic_Eraser) and [Super Nintendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_Dreamers) games, built on such lofty franchises as Sonic the Hedgehog and Chrono Trigger, that were only available over-the-air, and as a result they were lost to time for upwards of a decade.

With that in mind, I should be thankful that my personal favorite "lost game" - the game I played, but then couldn't - doesn't fall into that category. It has the luxury of at least being an offline PC game, with its files readily accessible... although, in the world of retro PC gaming, that's often less than half the battle.

# Retro PC Gaming

Personal computing is pretty close to homogeneous right now. While the x86 consensus of 2005-20 is broken again (thanks Apple), and certain things aren't achievable without a CUDA core handy (thanks NVIDIA), the relative popularity of open and/or cross-platform software frameworks combined with [a certain major game distributor's patronage of a certain compatibility layer](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/wine) have made it reasonable to assume that a program might, with [various amounts of fiddling](https://developer.apple.com/games/game-porting-toolkit/), can be run on any modern PC. 

This is a fairly new development. The x86/ARM divide is nothing compared to the wilderness of the 1990s. Intel's x86 processors (and compatible [clones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMD#IBM_PC_and_the_x86_architecture)) weren't the only CISC players, duking it out with Motorola's venerable 68000 family found in Amigas, Macs, and NeXT workstations. There were many RISC architectures competing with each other, including Apple/IBM's PowerPC (found in later Macs and Amigas before somehow [dominating the console space in the 2000s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_PowerPC-based_game_consoles)), Sun Microsystems' SPARC, and of course, the ARM processors in Acorn's Archimedes and Risc PC. (This is when the "A" in "ARM" still stood for "Acorn".) Even the implied promise that "x86 at least means IBM-compatible" was flouted by NEC's PC-98. (Whatever NEC did, they did it right, because [I haven't seen any anime love-letters to game development for the Apple II](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yhYCblIxfU0)).

That's just processors and operating systems. Outside of text adventures, sound and vision are critical elements of video games. 

## Graphics

Of course, there was no industry-wide standard for how to draw images or produce sound. Before we even get to the process of rendering an image, we need to talk about the different computer display standards. These were how computers were expected to "talk" to monitors. By the '90s, manufacturers had their pick of CGA, EGA, and VGA as standards for outputting to a computer monitor. Thus, different integrated and discrete graphics cards used different display standards; and that's before you consider the proprietary rendering features of graphics cards like 3dfx's Voodoo and NVIDIA's RIVA, which were among the first consumer-facing 3D graphics cards. Here's a video from Lukeno52 comparing *Lombard RAC Rally* in two display modes.

### An Aside: DirectX and OpenGL/Vulkan

On MS-DOS, there were no "graphics drivers". If a game supported a certain display standard, and a certain graphics card, it would talk directly to the graphics card at the hardware level. On one hand, this allowed for great flexibility; on the other hand, developers would need to support every possible permutation of graphics card and display standard. This meant that not all IBM-compatible games necessarily supported all IBM-compatible computers; for example, if you had a EGA display and the game was meant for VGA, you would be out of luck (although many games could fall back to CGA, if that option was palatable).

[Windows changed this, initially for the worse](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/a-whirlwind-tour-of-wing), with the operating system turning into a massive bottleneck between the game and the hardware. While Windows itself ran and looked consistent on all the hardware it supported, games could no longer talk directly to the graphics card API. This is why many games, even into the late 90s, still ran on MS-DOS; developers simply weren't willing to compromise performance. This led to an ever-widening gap where DOS games looked and performed far better than anything running natively on Windows. Microsoft's DirectX, which launched with Windows 95, was an attempt to solve this problem; present a unified API for 2D and 3D graphics, as well as sound, which would provide access to the hardware. Once DirectX caught on, it not only unlocked gaming on Windows, but it also helped solve the problem of game developers figuring out which graphics card and display mode to support. Games were easier to play on more computers.

DirectX wasn't the first attempt at a unified graphics API; [Silicon Graphics, Inc's OpenGL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenGL) was popular through its existence, and was supported by many platforms, before eventually being deprecated in favor of Vulkan. [Apple also does its own thing.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_(API))

It's also worth mentioning that a champion of DirectX at Microsoft - in fact, the developer who ported DOOM, PC gaming's killer app, to DirectX to showcase Windows 95 - was [Gabe Newell](https://www.pcgamer.com/history-direct-x-windows-microsoft/), who would later go on to found Valve Corporation, the sponsor of the most popular Wine distribution, Proton, which I mentioned above. Wine is a compatibility layer that allows Windows programs to be run on Linux without emulation, and it is the reason we can have a viable Linux-based handheld gaming machine in the form of the Steam Deck.

## Sound

By the 1980s, it was a reasonable expectation for a computer to be able to draw images on an electronic display of some sort. Sound, on the other hand, was considered a specialized use case. Many PCs of the 1980s had no sound, or were limited to the primitive [PC speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_speaker), a single small speaker built into the computer primarily meant for relaying diagnostic information from the boot-up process via beeps if video wasn't available. (In the days before every motherboard had [POST LEDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-on_self-test#Progress_and_error_reporting) in them, this was a practical option.) Eventually, some innovative developers figured out how to send varying levels and lengths of signal to the speaker using a technique called [pulse-width modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse-width_modulation), allowing for programs to play very rudimentary audio. 

This is how we got the "beeps and boops" style of music often associated with early retro gaming; the earliest arcade machines used a similar technique. Although a clever composer could arrange some [undeniably intricate music for the format](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d63s8BxzQp8), it had obvious limitations. Getting even a facsimile of a real instrument, let alone playing recognizable pre-recorded audio, was out of the question. PC speakers also weren't built to be fine-tuned, consistent instruments; differences in size and material would mean PC speakers would often play tones in inconsistent keys, timbres, or dynamic ranges. 

It was, of course, possible to turn digital files into analog signals meant for a high-fidelity speaker. [Digital-to-analog-converters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital-to-analog_converter) are the core of CD players, and have been common in consumer hardware since the early 1980s. They use [Pulse-code modulation, or PCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse-code_modulation), a method to transform digital information into sounds. However, the amount of storage space needed to hold a decent-sounding PCM sound file was fairly considerable. The [Compact Disc Digital Audio standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Disc_Digital_Audio) calls for 2 channels of LPCM audio, each signed 16-bit values, sampled at 44100 Hz. This adds up to about 70 minutes of music filling about 650 megabytes. (Lowering the bit depth or sampling rate would take up less space, at the cost of fidelity.) For a non-re-writable disc pressed at scale, this isn't a huge deal, but re-writable storage in the 1980s was far more expensive. Take for example the [Apple ProFile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_ProFile), which stored just five megabytes at the cost of $3,500 USD. (That's in 1981 dollars; adjusted for inflation, that's closer to $13,000 USD at time of writing, or roughly half the cost of the cheapest new car you can buy). 600 megabytes of hard drive capacity was prohibitively expensive for most consumers. While there were some [consumer-focused DACs for computers](https://crossfire-designs.de/index.php?lang=en&what=articles&name=showarticle.htm&article=soundcards&page=5), they were sparsely adopted.

However, there was a cost-effective middle ground between driving primitive PC speakers and storing massive audio files. What if, instead of storing a whole song, the computer were to store only a recording of one instrument, playing one note - and then, separately, a much smaller file instructing the computer to play certain notes in a specific order? The result would take up far less disk space, and still sound pretty good. Of course, I'm describing the [sampler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampler_(musical_instrument)), which was developed through the 1970s and a regular sight in a recording studio by the mid-1980s. These were also integrated into "pure" synthesizers, with the [Korg M1](https://vintagetechnologyarchive.com/synth/korg/m1/) being a prime example of a synthesizer that blended a PCM recording with a synthesized instrument, resulting in a much richer, organic sound. As samplers became smaller and cheaper, incorporating one as part of a home computer became increasingly practical.

The question of "how do we make computers operate samplers" was largely answered by the early 1980s, when the [MIDI standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIDI) was adopted as the de-facto way to send control signals to electronic instruments - be they analog synthesizers or digital samplers. The idea: a sequencer stored information about the musical composition (note pitch, timing, velocity, etc), which would be a relatively small file. It would transmit that to the instrument, which would follow the sequencer's instructions and play. This ended a brief but brutal format war between synthesizer manufacturers, where proprietary standards kept electronic musicians from easily moving between, say, Oberheim and Roland hardware.

The MIDI specification is maintained and respected [to this day](https://midi.org/specs). An important part of that standard is the [General MIDI Instrument list](https://soundprogramming.net/file-formats/general-midi-instrument-list/); a list of agreed-upon instrument sounds (be it digital sample or synthesizer facsimile) that should correspond to instrument numbers in a MIDI file. (There's a similar [drum kit list](https://soundprogramming.net/file-formats/general-midi-drum-note-numbers/)). What this means is that, if a MIDI instrument receives a note meant for instrument "one", it knew it was supposed to at least try to sound like an acoustic grand piano. Instrument "twenty-four", meanwhile, is meant to sound like a tango accordion.

All of this - DACs, MIDI, and samplers - finally brings us to the [sound card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_card). By the end of the 1980s, different manufacturers were shipping expansion cards for PCs that included DACs, as well as on-board synthesizers that could play sounds that provided a reasonable facsimile of instruments as called for in the General MIDI standard. [While they used different chips, and had different amounts of channels and provided different effects](https://crossfire-designs.de/index.php?lang=en&what=articles&name=showarticle.htm&article=soundcards&page=7), the result was a cost-effective, high-fidelity musical experience.

It's important to stress that, despite General MIDI providing guidance on what instruments should sound like, the actual timbre on these sound cards was not identical from one model to another. Sound cards had varying numbers of channels and effects, and implemented different instruments in different ways. There were expensive sound cards and cheap sound cards; cheap ones may have less channels, and be pure synthesizers with no PCM audio samples, whereas expensive ones may have many channels and rich instrument patches with high-quality PCM samples. Many games even had different sound modes meant for different sound cards, which would impact the number of channels or effects used by a song. As a result, your choice of sound card (and if your game supported it) would have a big impact on your game. Like the video standards described prior, some games fell back to a "lowest common denominator" approach, sacrificing the special features of some cards to ensure compatibility with all of them.

In other words, just like with video, games might handle sound very differently depending on your hardware. Here's a diagram of some scenarios where sound might get better, worse, or go away entirely.

    B -->|PC speaker and a Roland LAPC-I |C(OK, so we can have sound.)
    C -->D(Do I use the features and channels of the Roland?)
    D -->|Yes|F(Here's some music using all the channels and effects of the Roland card)

# A Final Unity

The proliferation of different operating systems, sound cards, graphics cards, and display standards in the 1980s and early 1990s meant that, even on the most common IBM-compatible PCs, every game ran different. On a game console, you had a reasonable guarantee that a game would play the same on any console you plugged it into, [the long-lived Sega Genesis/Mega Drive's mid-lifecycle sound chip swap notwithstanding](https://consolemods.org/wiki/Genesis:Audio_Chip_Notes). On a PC, however, you had to be a bit of an enthusiast to know if your game would play at all, let alone [well enough to be worth the investment in your hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/video-games/borderlands-4-launch-marred-by-performance-issues). "Literally unplayable" was not an ironic statement back then. 

On the flip side, though, a game targeting a good PC, with a good video card and a good sound card, would eclipse anything a contemporary game console could play.

It was during the tail end of this era that I had my introduction to PC gaming. My first gaming memory is playing a shareware version of [Lemmings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemmings_(video_game)) for MS-DOS on my dad's IBM ThinkPad, which was running Windows 3.1 and unceremoniously shed the desktop environment to play the game (as most games did at the time). 

Some time later, we'd have our first "family computer"; an IBM-compatible (I don't remember if it was a Packard-Bell, a Gateway 2000, or one of the other beige boxes from the era). It shipped with Windows 95, including the demo disk that showcased the machine's multimedia capabilities by including a whole music video by an [up-and-coming band from Los Angeles](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hqi2Jy0UMiA). Over our 56k modem (and, later, 128kbps ISDN line), we explored the walled garden of America Online, downloading demos and shareware games, usually of the simple puzzler variety, but sometimes buying games showcasing the [stunning full-motion video and realistic audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YFdDiFPQ2sw) you could only fit on a gigantic 600MB CD-ROM (or three of them).

Somewhere along the line, probably around 1997, I got a copy of a game called [Star Trek: A Final Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek%3A_The_Next_Generation_%E2%80%93_A_Final_Unity). It had come out in 1995, during the era when any games with decent graphics were targeting MS-DOS instead of wrangling Windows' difficult graphics API. Launching it was a chore; I'd need to open an MS-DOS prompt, navigate to the folder I installed it in, and launch it from the command line. If I wanted to configure the game, I'd run a whole different program, which would act as a "friendly" wizard to configure a .ini file. With some guidance from my parents, I was able to navigate the terminal for the first time (as an eight-year-old, I was among the first cohorts that grew up using a mouse). I was able to play the game.

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/BQwnmnenPt4" title="A playthrough video of Star Trek: A Final Unity on MS-DOS.." frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

*A Final Unity* is a point-and-click adventure/puzzle game, in the vein of what LucasArts and Sierra On-Line had been making throughout the decade; the gameplay largely centered around reading dialogue, obtaining items, using items on objects in the environment, and picking the right answers to questions. (There is a real-time space combat component of the game, although it is kludgy enough that the best move is usually to give the conn to Lieutenant Worf, who can "autopilot" you through these segments; you can also handle damage control and routing power to engines and weapons [ala X-Wing](https://youtu.be/xoBFPvIxcI8?t=116), but again, that is best delegated to Lt. Commander La Forge. The gameplay is there, though, so points for that.)

The graphics were brilliant, colorful representations of the world of *Star Trek: The Next Generation*. [Pre-rendered 3D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-rendering) and photo-realistic graphics in high-resolution 800x600 (that's [better than TV!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTSC)). Real-time scaling allowed 2D objects like characters and ships to move closer and farther from the screen, allowing for a 3D-like effect, which was especially impressive during the aforementioned [combat sections](https://youtu.be/BQwnmnenPt4?t=8618). Although it wasn't true 3D, it allowed for more detailed textures that gave a more cinematic look than [true 3D space sims of the time](https://youtu.be/vQjj-hUGnYE?t=7123). (I need to stress that, at this point in time, just being able to make a low-poly model with no lighting or textures move around at 10fps was [not just acceptable, but considered pretty good](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a0edFbcH1VM&t=241s).)

The pacing could be a bit better; probably double-digits of gameplay time could be spent watching characters walk at their own pace across the game's sprawling landscapes, [especially on Morrasia](https://youtu.be/es03Jd38LfY?&t=355), and there's at least one [notorious puzzle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tCAJ4ARVrMA&list=PL66033A48E4A455EB&index=19) that ended my run in the pre-GameFAQs era. That said, having never played any of the aforementioned SCUMM point-and-click classics, the rush of finding a new interactable object or starting a new interaction was incredibly satisfying. 

I haven't mentioned what may be the most noteworthy feature of the game yet. As can be gleaned from the YouTube videos I've linked, all of the dialogue in this game was voiced by the cast of Star Trek: The Next Generation. As I mentioned before, storing and reproducing PCM audio on a computer was something of a luxury as late as the mid-90s, so having an entire episode's worth of voice acting for each character, including bewildered variations of "I can't use that there" for each member of the Enterprise-D's senior staff, was a thrilling bonus. It really was like playing the show.

*A Final Unity* wasn't the only Star Trek game to do this. Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, it was common for Star Trek games to feature the voice talents of the shows' casts - and, in the case of Interplay's fantastic *Starfleet/Klingon Academy* games, reprise their roles on-screen for the FMV interludes in the X-Wing clones. In fact, I believe DeForest Kelley's final appearance as [Dr. McCoy was in 1995](https://youtu.be/ZcgE3qZ9uws?t=762), well after his last on-screen appearance in Star Trek VI. Original Series actors portrayed the crew of the NCC-1701 long after the "TOS/Movie" era was considered to be over, with George Takei and Walter Koenig sending off Sulu and Chekov [on the PS2 in 2004](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c1QzD7rdf8w), and William Shatner brought [Captain Kirk to the Xbox 360](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OdKFl5bU9oA) more than ten years after his demise in Star Trek: Generations. (I'd like to imagine that someone got *Star Trek: Legacy* and *Sonic the Hedgehog (2006)* under the Christmas tree that year, and was radicalized into a sort of millennial James Rolfe.)

This brilliant facet of '90s Trek ephemera was lost on me, unfortunately, as *A Final Unity* was trying to output the PCM audio to a non-existent Gravis Ultrasound instead of whatever sound device came with my family's PC. (Remember, it was an MS-DOS game, and had no use for Windows' multimedia APIs.) As a result, I could hear the game's MIDI soundcheck, but the voices were tantalizingly out of reach.

I gave up on the game after getting stuck on that [accursed Orchestrion puzzle on Frigis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tCAJ4ARVrMA&list=PL66033A48E4A455EB&index=19). The years rolled by, and I eventually lost track of that computer, as well as my copy of the game. I never forgot it, though. Over the years, emulation of IBM-compatibles became increasingly trivial thanks to the hard work of the folks behind [Dosbox](https://www.dosbox.com/index.php), and the fantastic fork [Dosbox-X](https://www.dosbox.com/index.php) which allows us to set up the hardware loadout of our DOS environment - down to the clock speed, graphics and sound cards, and processor. Every now and then, I would check GOG (back when it was explicitly Good Old Games), and as their selection of '90s sci-fi games began to widen, I hoped to see *A Final Unity* appear. Sadly, it never did. Eventually I decided to track down a copy on eBay.

In late 2025, I was playing this game again, this time on a 2022 Lenovo laptop running Ubuntu 24.04. With the game (correctly) targeting the emulated Creative Sound Blaster for PCM audio (and using FluidSynth for MIDI), the game finally got to shine. With the help of YouTube walkthroughs like the ones I linked earlier, I made it past the dreaded orchestrion puzzle on Frigis. I got to enjoy more puzzles, more deadpan dialogue from the TNG cast, and finally got to see the game's mysterious villains, who had only been alluded to at until late-game; the fantastic [Chodak](https://memory-beta.fandom.com/wiki/Chodak), who deserve a re-appraisal for their fantastic design.

## Losing the Digital Canon

(When I say "canon", I am of course referring to the "body of high art held in high value" definition of the word, not the "accepted as being part of a continuity" definition. Sorry, MicroProse. We still love the Chodak.)

[Current trends](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-26-gartner-says-surging-memory-costs-will-reduce-global-pc-and-smartphone-shipments-in-2026) aside, computer data storage has been getting [exponentially cheaper over time](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/historical-cost-of-computer-memory-and-storage), and digital art doesn't require the kind of physical space, conditions, and care that physical media [needs](https://russell-collection.com/how-to-store-oil-paintings/) to survive. One would think we've entered an era where art, like everything else we do on the computer, can never be forgotten. Instead, we're entering an era where art is more - not less - likely to be lost to time. 

As I explained above, appreciating a computer game in the proper context requires much more than a copy of the game files. Reproducing a game like *Star Trek: A Final Unity* on a modern computer required countless hours of (often volunteer) developers understanding the graphics, sound, operating system, and hardware components of obsolete computers which, in turn, took countless hours to develop, based off of decades of evolution and innovation. This game is lucky; it was released on the most dominant operating system (MS-DOS), running on the most dominant processor architecture (the 8086 family). It was also released on physical media. Lots of equally deserving games were released targeting niche platforms that may still not have accurate emulation, or encrypted with DRM software, or only runs with an internet connection - or, even worse, never released physically at all.

The clock is ticking to preserve these games. The companies that made them often weren't looking forward to preserving them for future generations; as companies and IPs changed hands, licenses were lost, and people were laid off, the games' source code and assets were often left on hard drives which were wiped or thrown out by people who may not have even known what was on them. The assets for the original StarCraft, despite having leaned heavily on the "pre-rendered 3D" art style that would have scaled up very well on a modern display, [were completely lost](https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2017/06/hands-on-with-starcraft-remastered-ahead-of-its-august-14-launch-for-15/). The same goes for [Crash Bandicoot](https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2017/06/a-remaster-with-no-old-code-crash-bandicoot-was-rebuilt-nearly-from-scratch/), the original PlayStation's flagship platformer. These losses were merely snags in AAA remasters of beloved IPs; countless more games are just lost.

This problem extends beyond video games. In the post-mixtape, post-CD world, music can just disappear. MySpace, a popular (and, often, the only) venue to host music by unsigned artists, [lost a generation of songs](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2019/03/myspace-apparently-lost-12-years-worth-of-music-and-almost-no-one-noticed/) during a botched server migration. Songs from 2003 to 2015 were lost with no hope for recovery. Bands in 2005 weren't uploading to Bandcamp, YouTube, or SoundCloud; MySpace was *it*. Many young musicians from that era probably didn't keep copies of their own music after a while, or if they did, they haven't deemed it necessary to distribute it again. Music that was formative to my development as a late-aughts teenager has been erased from history. Not out of print, not relegated to illegal means of reproduction - these songs are *gone*. If you're lucky, one of the best songs you heard in high school might exist in flip-phone quality, until that inevitably gets lost to the sands of time. If you're lucky.

In college, I was always amazed by the microfiche; detailed copies of periodicals dating back over a century could be preserved in a cabinet, ready to be made available at a moment's notice. If I needed to know if something happened on a certain day, or find a corroborating primary source for something, I was sure that with enough time and effort, I'd find my answer in an old newspaper.

Pew Research has a [grim statistic](https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2024/05/17/when-online-content-disappears/) that 38% of web pages from 2013 are no longer accessible. *This includes primary sources, like the news!* This can be because of the expense of running a website, or [misguided SEO initiatives](https://gizmodo.com/cnet-deletes-thousands-old-articles-google-search-seo-1850721475). Perhaps worse, offline sites can have their backlog purchased, [AI-slopified, desecrated and repurposed as a place to hang ads](https://www.404media.co/a-beloved-tech-blog-tuaw-is-now-publishing-ai-articles-under-the-names-of-its-old-human-staff/). Either way, the result is the same; primary sources are destroyed without recourse. We are losing history.

There are organizations that are doing their best to preserve what they can; [Stop Killing Games](https://www.stopkillinggames.com/en#about) is pushing hard on preservation and consumers' rights. Internet Archive has done what it can, despite [legal challenges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Archive#Copyright_issues). The most we can hope for is that organizations like these, and preservationists who are able to keep this software extant and usable, will eventually staunch the bleeding.

I'm not sure what my conclusion is, other than - hoard your data. An old hard drive can be as important as an old photo book. You won't know what you've lost until long after you've lost it.

Also, open standards are important. Open means it can be re-implemented somewhere, some other time. Closed standards are a death sentence for anything using them.
